What I do

Two jobs, at different scales.

I assess and rehabilitate cricket injuries, and I help teams and organisations build the systems that decide whether players stay available. Those two jobs sound separate. They are the same job at different scales.

Most of my week is spent one to one with cricketers, in person in Richmond or on video with players elsewhere. The rest is consulting work: injury surveillance, workload systems, rehabilitation pathways, and reviews of how a sports science and sports medicine department operates.

How I work

Three things shape every plan.

Availability is the outcome that matters

A player who is fit in the gym and unavailable on Saturday has not been rehabilitated. So the plan works backwards from the match: what the role demands, what the schedule looks like, and what has to be true before you take the field.

Simple systems survive a season

Elaborate programs look thorough and fail quietly. Players stop doing them in week three, usually without telling anyone. I would rather give you four things you will do than fourteen you will not.

Evidence matters, context decides

Research tells you what tends to happen across groups. It does not tell you what to do with a bowler who has one match left in a season and a contract in question. I will tell you what the evidence supports, where it runs out, and what I would do.

Cricket, from the other side

I have had my own surgery and rehab.

It is a useful thing for a physiotherapist to have been through. It shortens the distance between what a plan looks like on paper and what it feels like at week six, when progress has stopped.

What I will not do

Honest limits.

  • Promise a timeline I cannot stand behind. Tissue heals at its own rate, and the honest answer to "how long" is usually a range with conditions attached.
  • Tell you an injury has one cause. Cricket injuries almost never do. Bowling load matters, and so do sleep, travel, schedule density, technique, previous injury and what happened in the gym.
  • Sell you a screen that claims to predict injury. No screening test does that reliably. Testing is useful for a baseline and for tracking change, which is a smaller and more honest claim.
