Teams and organisations

Fewer players unavailable, for reasons you understand

Most squads do not have an injury problem. They have a decision problem, a communication problem or a workload problem that shows up as injuries.

Discuss your programme

Where availability goes

When a squad carries more injuries than it should, the cause is rarely a missing piece of equipment or a treatment nobody has tried. It is usually one of these:

  • Nobody knows what the bowlers have bowled. Load is estimated from memory, which flatters everyone.
  • Nobody owns the return-to-play decision. Coach, physiotherapist and player each assume someone else has it, so it defaults to whoever is most confident in the room.
  • Rehabilitation ends at pain-free. Players return without being tested against the demands of the role, and the reinjury is treated as bad luck.
  • Information dies at handovers. Between seasons, between staff, between the club and the pathway. Every new person starts from zero.
  • The monitoring exists but nobody fills it in. Which is worse than none, because the gaps look like zeroes.

None of these are solved by buying software. They are solved by deciding who decides what, writing it down, and making the system small enough that busy people use it.

What I do

Injury surveillance and workload systems

Set up recording that people will keep up with, in a form that answers the questions you keep asking: who is climbing too fast, who has missed too much, and what happened in the four weeks before this injury. Built to fit the staffing you have, not the staffing a manual assumes.

Rehabilitation and return-to-play pathways

Written criteria for the injuries that cost your squad the most cricket, so the return decision is made against a standard rather than against whoever is most persuasive. Includes who owns each decision and what happens when people disagree.

Sports science and sports medicine department reviews

A structured look at how the department runs: structure, communication flow, injury trends, reporting, monitoring, staffing, scheduling, rehabilitation pathways, data use and the cultural dynamics underneath all of it. The output is a report with prioritised, costed recommendations, not a list of everything that is imperfect.

Emerging cricket nations

Work with associate and developing programmes, where the constraint is resources rather than knowledge. The answer there is almost never the system a full member uses in miniature. It is a smaller system designed for the staffing and budget that exist, which is a different piece of design work. I do this as Head of Sports Science and Sports Medicine with the Federazione Cricket Italiana.

Tournament and tour support

Planning, medical logistics and on-tour physiotherapy, including environments where the local support is thin and the documentation matters more than the equipment.

How I work

I start by asking what you have already tried and why it stopped. That conversation usually tells me more than a data export does.

Then the smallest thing that would help. A squad with no load monitoring does not need a performance platform. It needs one shared sheet that everyone fills in, running for a season, before anyone discusses software. Staging beats scaling: systems that grow out of use survive, systems bought in advance get abandoned.

I will tell you when the honest answer is that you do not need me. A consultant who finds a large problem in every organisation is not assessing, they are selling.

One thing I will not do

Blame practitioners. Almost every dysfunctional medical department I have seen was full of capable people inside a structure that made good work difficult: reporting lines that put the physiotherapist under the person picking the side, contracts too short to think past the season, no protected time for rehabilitation. Reviews that name individuals are easy and change nothing.
